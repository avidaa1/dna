01 · THE MOLECULE

A double helix you can take apart

Two antiparallel sugar-phosphate backbones winding around a shared axis, with base pairs stacked between them. Every element is separable: toggle a base type off and watch which rungs vanish, or click a rung and the narrator describes that pairing, its hydrogen bonds and its position along the axis. The READOUT panel reports the geometry actually measured from whichever model is loaded — turns and base pairs per turn are fitted from the backbone, not assumed.

Three models, only one to scale

Schematic is a scanned 32-pair model with every base resolved by colour. Atomic is a ball-and-stick build of 37 pairs — spheres for atom centres, sticks for bonds, no base identity assigned. Both are artists’ models: fitting their backbones gives roughly 31 and 25 base pairs per turn with the strands about 180 degrees apart, so they are stylised, loosely wound and effectively symmetric. Synthetic is generated live from your sequence on real B-form constants — 10.5 pairs per turn, 0.34 nm rise, strands offset 140 degrees — and is the only one of the three whose proportions and grooves are correct.

Two strands, opposite directions

One strand runs 5′→3′, the other 3′→5′. That antiparallel arrangement is not decorative — it is why replication is continuous on one strand and fragmented on the other, and why polymerases only ever build in a single direction.

ANTIPARALLEL5′ TO 3′

Complementary pairing

Adenine binds thymine with two hydrogen bonds. Guanine binds cytosine with three. A purine always faces a pyrimidine, which keeps the helix a constant 2 nanometres wide along its entire length.

A=T · 2HG≡C · 3H

Grooves are addresses

In real DNA the backbones are not diametrically opposite, so the helix carries a wide major groove and a narrow minor groove. Regulatory proteins read chemical patterns in the major groove without ever unzipping the strands. Turn on GROOVES to trace both channels — on a model whose strands sit near 180 degrees apart the tracks render in neutral grey, because there is no genuine major or minor to distinguish.

MAJOR 2.2 nmMINOR 1.2 nm

Geometry of B-DNA

Ten and a half base pairs per full turn, 0.34 nanometres of rise per pair, a 3.4 nanometre pitch, and a right-handed twist. Those four numbers describe almost every DNA molecule in a living cell — and they are what the synthesizer builds. Compare the fitted BP / TURN in the readout as you switch models.

10.5 bp / TURNRIGHT-HANDED

Not the only form

Dehydrated fibres adopt A-DNA: shorter, fatter, still right-handed. Alternating purine-pyrimidine tracts can flip into left-handed Z-DNA, whose zig-zag backbone appears transiently behind active transcription.

A-FORMZ-FORM

Scale

A single human cell holds roughly two metres of DNA compressed into a nucleus about six micrometres across. Laid end to end, the DNA in one adult body would span the solar system many times over.

04 · MUTATION ENGINE

Break it on purpose

Mutation is not an abstraction — it is one character changing in a string billions of characters long. The engine applies a mutation to your sequence, recompiles the protein, compares it to the original and classifies the damage.

Apply a mutation

CLASSIFIES SILENT / MISSENSE / NONSENSE / FRAMESHIFT AGAINST THE ORIGINAL TRANSLATION

Awaiting mutation.

Silent

The base changes, the codon changes, the amino acid does not. The genetic code is redundant — six different codons all specify leucine. Most third-position changes are silent.

Missense

One amino acid is replaced by another. Sickle cell disease is a single adenine becoming thymine in the beta-globin gene, turning glutamate into valine at position six.

Nonsense

A codon becomes a stop signal and the protein is truncated mid-build. The fragment is usually degraded before it can fold, so the gene is effectively switched off.

Frameshift

Insertions or deletions that are not multiples of three shift every downstream codon. Everything after the mutation is read wrong. These are typically the most destructive of all.

05 · GADGET · ELECTROPHORESIS

Run your digest on a gel

Cut the sequence with real enzymes, then separate the pieces the way a bench does it. DNA is uniformly negative because of its phosphate backbone, so an electric field drags every fragment toward the positive electrode — and the agarose mesh holds the big ones back. Small fragments run far, large fragments barely move. Migration distance tracks the logarithm of fragment length, which is why the ladder rungs crowd together at the bottom.

Why it separates at all

Every nucleotide carries one negative phosphate, so charge scales with length just as mass does. The charge-to-mass ratio is effectively constant, which means the field alone cannot sort the fragments. The sieving effect of the gel matrix is doing all the work.

Reading a ladder

A ladder is a mixture of known lengths run alongside the sample. You size an unknown band by where it lands between two ladder rungs. Because migration is logarithmic, estimates in the crowded lower region are much less precise than they look.

Voltage is a trade

Higher voltage finishes the run sooner but generates heat and lets fragments diffuse sideways, so bands smear and close sizes stop resolving. Lower percentage agarose opens the mesh for long fragments; higher percentage tightens it for short ones.

Seeing the DNA

DNA is colourless. An intercalating dye slides between the stacked bases and fluoresces orange under ultraviolet light, so the bands only appear once the lamp is on. That dye is a mutagen precisely because it inserts itself into the helix.
